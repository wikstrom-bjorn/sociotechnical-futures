# Research question

**As AI agents take over decisions previously made directly by individuals — what to buy, what to
schedule, what to answer, what to prioritize — what happens to human agency, and to the legitimacy of
the institutions and markets those agents now mediate?**

Sub-questions this case tries to answer:

1. Is agentic delegation experienced by users as an extension of their own agency, or as a
   displacement of it — and does that experience change as agents become more autonomous (fewer
   confirmation steps, higher-value decisions)?
2. Who absorbs the accountability gap when an agent acts "on someone's behalf" but the outcome is
   contested — the user, the platform, the agent developer, or nobody?
3. Do institutions (regulators, consumer-protection bodies, employers) retain legitimacy if their
   actual counterpart in a transaction or decision is increasingly another organization's AI agent
   rather than the person they believe they're serving?
4. What is the 2030 steady state: agentic delegation as a normalized convenience layer, a contested
   and regulated infrastructure, or a partially rolled-back experiment?

## Why this framing, not a broader one

This case deliberately does not ask "how capable will AI agents be by 2030" (a capability-forecasting
question, not a foresight-about-society question). It asks what happens to agency and legitimacy
*given* the adoption trajectory already visible in 2025–2026 — because that trajectory is the signal
this method starts from, not a prediction to be argued separately.
